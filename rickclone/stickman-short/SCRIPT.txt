She walked into a street one summer morning and began to dance, and did not stop for six days. You see, she wasn't celebrating anything, and within weeks a hundred people were dancing beside her, then four hundred. Some of them danced until they dropped, begging anyone to make it stop. Nobody could. So the city first tried to shut it down: no dancing, and the worst dancers locked away. That failed, so the doctors examined them, stroked their beards, and named the cause: hot blood. The cure, everyone agreed, was more dancing. The guild hall opened its doors. Stages went up in the market square, and musicians were paid to play. The goal was to dance the sickness clean out of them, and whole crowds came to help. Which is also how the city doubled its problem. By September, it ended the only way plagues end: it simply faded, and the stages came down for firewood. And here is the part everyone missed. You see, the harvest before this had failed, and the winter that followed was one of the cruelest anyone remembered, so the whole city was starving, frightened, and one bad week from the edge. Nobody danced because they were happy. Their bodies carried them along, and fear and hunger picked up the bill. The music was never the cure. The music was the kindling.
