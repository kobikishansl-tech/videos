## Every fact, and where it is

- A woman (studied under the name Frau Troffea) began dancing in a Strasbourg street in July 1518 and
  danced for about six days without rest — Strasbourg chronicles; John Waller, *A Time to Dance, a Time
  to Die* (2009), ch. 1.
- Within about a month up to four hundred people were dancing — city chronicle counts for August 1518
  (Waller, 2009, ch. 1; the number 400 is the standard figure).
- Dancers begged bystanders to help them stop; some collapsed; contemporary chronicles report deaths at
  the peak (the famous "fifteen a day" is a later claim, disputed, so the narration only says they dropped).
- The council's recorded response: dancers were banned, some were carted away; a pilgrimage to the shrine
  of Sankt Sabinus in the Vosges was organised — Waller (2009); Strasbourg council records.
- The university-trained physicians' diagnosis recorded in the sources: "hot blood" (a natural, not
  supernatural, cause) — Waller (2009), citing the chronicles; also the account of Paracelsus' later visit.
- The council authorised the guild halls to be opened, stages to be built and musicians to be hired —
  the only remedy they had settled on; recorded in the council minutes (Waller, 2009, ch. 5).
- The dancing faded out by September and the stages were dismantled — chronicles.
- 1516's harvest had failed and the winter of 1516-17 was exceptionally cold; Strasbourg in 1518 was
  hungry, crowded and afraid (leading to processions and fear of a curse) — the famine context is the
  standard historian's account (Waller, 2009, ch. 4) and the magistrates' own year-end records.
- The short must NOT imply: a supernatural curse was real, the dancing was joyful, ergot poisoning is the
  settled explanation (it is contested), or that a named person (Troffea) is the only dancer whose name survives.
